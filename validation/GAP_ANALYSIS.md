**Team:** Srinath Venkatesh, Chantrice Santiago, Kanishka Gupta, Sneha Vyas

## Overview

This document maps observed failures from the prompting study (Claude, Copilot, Gemini) and speed-dating interviews (8 participants, numbered as in [INTERVIEW_INDEX.md](INTERVIEW_INDEX.md)) to the Gonzalez et al. cognitive pillars. Each row follows the chain: **Receipt (evidence) → Theory (why it matters) → Design (how to fix it).**

## Dimension 1: Accuracy & Hallucinations

**Theme:** AI generates plausible-sounding products, prices, and nutrition without verifying sources. Users lose trust because they cannot interrogate the data origin.

| Evidence | Observed Failure | Theoretical Interpretation | Design Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gemini P1** ([Gemini_raw_outputs.md, line 727](transcripts/raw-outputs/Gemini_raw_outputs.md)): Banana price listed as $1.99 vs. catalog $2.49; pizza as single-pack $6.99 vs. catalog four-pack $13.99. | AI invents catalog products, packages, and prices without verifying inventory. | **Memory (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "AI systems offer the potential to strengthen collective memory by serving as knowledge amplifiers, curators, and organizers." However, "AI's prodigious memory is only as useful as its accuracy, sourcing, and currency. Language models may hallucinate or present outdated information with confidence, making human oversight essential to verify and contextualize machine-provided knowledge." Hallucinated prices destroy the shared understanding of what products exist and what they cost. | **Validate all products, packages, and prices against a trusted source (USDA FoodData Central or supplied catalog) before presenting to user.** Never show invented products. Attach a **provenance tag** to every price: "Confirmed from Costco inventory (Sept 30)" vs. "Estimated based on similar items (±10%)." Never present an estimate as an exact price. |
| **Copilot P1** ([Copilot_raw_outputs.md, line 280–328](transcripts/raw-outputs/Copilot_raw_outputs.md)): Returns a cart estimate of $295.77 without showing a seven-day meal plan, line items, or nutrition. | AI provides a high-level cost estimate without supporting detail. User cannot interrogate why it costs so much. | **Interrogation Orchestration (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Empirical research shows that teams perform best when humans actively interrogate AI recommendations rather than passively accepting them." A bare cost estimate invites skepticism and passive acceptance. Users need to see meals, ingredients, and individual prices to trust the total. | **Always provide itemized output:** meal table, daily nutrition totals, ingredient list with serving sizes, cart rows with unit prices. Never return a bare estimate without supporting detail. |
| **Interview 3 (Chantrice, UIUC MSTM grad student):** "If something says 20 minutes and actually takes an hour, I'm not going to use it again." | Cooking time estimates are wrong or unsupported; one bad estimate ends user engagement. | **Reasoning (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Humans provide contextual judgment about feasibility" and recognize when an AI recommendation doesn't fit their life. An unrealistic cooking time signals that the AI doesn't understand real-world constraints, breaking the shared mental model of what "feasible" means. One trust violation can end the relationship. | **Label every cooking time by how it was checked:** *AI-estimated* (the model's figure), *heuristic minimum applied* (raised to a realistic floor for slow foods, e.g. raw rice or chicken ≥ 15 min), or *source-verified* (matched to cited recipe sources; future work). Never call a heuristic-adjusted time verified. Add a **confidence toggle** so users can filter out AI-estimated times. |
| **Gemini P5** ([Gemini_raw_outputs.md, line 1898](transcripts/raw-outputs/Gemini_raw_outputs.md)): Inventory lacks a curry kit; Gemini invents its price ($14.99), nutrition (380 kcal, 28g protein), and allergen status, then includes it in Day 4 and the cart. | AI acknowledges missing data, labels the values "estimated," then serves the invented product as if it were verified. | **Knowledge Infrastructure (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Hybrid knowledge systems in which AI retrieves, organizes, and cross-references information while humans validate, contextualize, and adjudicate are emerging as a robust model for organizational learning." Labeling invented data as "estimated" does not make it acceptable in a safety-critical context (allergies). Users cannot verify ungrounded nutrition or allergen claims. | **Hard constraint:** If a recipe ingredient is not in the verified database, **do not serve the meal.** Escalate: "Curry kit (C-unlisted) is not in our database. Options: (A) substitute with verified Indian curry (C19), (B) skip this meal, (C) you provide product details (nutrition, allergens)." Never guess. |

## Dimension 2: Reliability & Consistency

**Theme:** AI detects conflicts but proceeds anyway; ingredient lists diverge from carts; locked preferences are re-optimized without approval. Users cannot rely on the plan staying consistent.

| Evidence | Observed Failure | Theoretical Interpretation | Design Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Claude P4** ([Claude_raw_outputs.md, line 629, 654, 666](transcripts/raw-outputs/Claude_raw_outputs.md)): Day 1 and Day 6 lentil lunches listed as "20–25 min" against a hard 20-minute limit. Claude flags this and asks for confirmation, but then presents the plan as "feasible" and "meets all targets." | AI detects a constraint violation, asks for user input, but then proceeds as if the conflict is resolved despite no explicit user approval. | **Reasoning and Meta-Coordination (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** Clear decision rights are essential. When AI flags a conflict and asks "Please confirm this fits your 20-minute limit," the user may interpret silence as acceptance, or the AI may interpret the flag as self-resolved. The result: a plan presented as complete that violates a hard constraint. Complementarity requires that **escalation points are explicit and approval is unambiguous.** | **Implement hard approval gates:** When a constraint is violated, pause the workflow with explicit options: "(A) Accept the 20–25 min overage on Days 1 & 6, (B) Remove lentil meals, (C) Increase time limit to 25 min." Wait for a choice; do not proceed on silence. Mark the plan as "conditional" until the user chooses. |
| **Copilot P2** ([Copilot_raw_outputs.md, line 367, 409–618](transcripts/raw-outputs/Copilot_raw_outputs.md)): Meal plan includes protein shakes on Days 2, 4, and 6 in recipes; cart has zero protein shakes. Summary says they were "omitted to control cost," but user did not approve this trade-off. | Ingredients disappear from cart without explanation. Plan and cart diverge; user cannot trust that meals are purchasable. | **Shared Mental Models (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Calibrated trust and accurate shared mental models of the AI's abilities and limitations" are essential. A divergence between meals and ingredients breaks the shared understanding of "what will I buy to execute this plan?" Users must see the consequence of cost-cutting before accepting it. | **Rebuild cart after every plan change.** Before presenting a final cart that differs from the planned meals, show: "To stay under $145, we removed: [protein shakes, X, Y]. Affected meals: [Day 2 breakfast, Day 4 lunch]. Replace with: [options]." Require explicit approval before finalizing. |
| **Interview 2 (Srinath, UIUC MS Engineering student):** "If I say chicken for lunch, don't change it later just because you're optimizing the rest of the plan." | User locks a preference; AI re-optimizes and changes or removes the locked item without re-approval. | **Meta-Coordination and Role Partitioning (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** Clear role boundaries are essential: "AI executes structured processes and upholds procedural reliability through monitoring, workflow coordination, and auditability. Humans design the overarching team architecture, including escalation paths, decision rights, and division of labor." Locked items are user-level decisions; re-optimization without re-approval violates the human's role as final authority. | **Implement a lock mechanism.** Allow users to lock meals, proteins, or budget. Display locked items prominently in UI. **Never re-optimize locked items** without explicit re-approval. When re-optimizing, show: "You locked: [chicken for lunch]. We're re-optimizing around this. New plan changes: [Y, Z]. Approve?" |
| **Gemini P4** ([Gemini_raw_outputs.md, line 1650, 1743](transcripts/raw-outputs/Gemini_raw_outputs.md)): Prompt specifies no-pantry inventory; Gemini responds "while utilizing non-perishable store-cupboard staples assumed to be on hand." Then claims products are "completely free of peanuts and tree nuts" without full ingredient data. | AI changes a hard constraint (no-pantry) and makes an unsupported safety claim. | **Reasoning and Accountability (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Humans must remain the locus of ethical authority and accountability in consequential decisions, while AI can strengthen collective reasoning by clarifying goals, surfacing assumptions, flagging misalignments, and generating alternative hypotheses." Hard constraints (allergies, no-pantry, dietary restrictions) are not suggestions; they are boundaries. Changing them without approval and making unsupported safety claims violates both reasoning and accountability. | **Treat allergies, dietary restrictions, and pantry status as immutable hard constraints.** If a plan cannot be built within them, escalate: "Cannot meet: 120g protein + $75 budget + no-cook + severe nut allergy. You must re-prioritize. Options: (A) increase budget, (B) lower protein target, (C) add 10 min cook time." Do not guess or assume. |

## Dimension 3: Latency & Performance

**Theme:** Response times vary widely; some users abandon on latency. Visible progress increases tolerance. Trade-off between speed and quality is not explicit.

| Evidence | Observed Failure | Theoretical Interpretation | Design Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Interview 4 (Chantrice, UIUC undergraduate):** Would wait about 10–15 seconds for a weekly plan; longer than that and they would use a general chatbot instead. | Slow response time causes abandonment; user switches to alternative tool. | **Attention (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Attention concerns how human–AI teams monitor information, handle exceptions, and allocate focus in dynamic environments." If the system doesn't deliver results within the user's attention window (10–15 s), the user stops paying attention and goes elsewhere. The system loses the opportunity to collaborate. | **Target 15–20 s for a full-week plan and cart.** Cache nutrition lookups (USDA queries). Run meal generation and cart consolidation in parallel, not sequentially. Show **visible progress:** "Checking pantry (1/4)... Generating meals (2/4)... Validating nutrition (3/4)... Building cart (4/4)." Visible progress raises latency tolerance. |
| **Interview 8 (Sneha, mid-career, family):** "I'd wait up to a minute for a good weekly plan. Saving me 30 minutes of planning is much more valuable than getting it in 10 seconds." | Different users have vastly different latency tolerance; one-size-fits-all speed target misses user needs. | **Attention Orchestration (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** Users allocate attention differently. Parents planning for a family value quality over speed. Young professionals value speed over depth. The system should expose this trade-off rather than optimize for one group. | **Add a quality vs. speed control:** Offer "Quick Mode" (≈15 s: less variety, fewer alternatives, shorter explanations) vs. "Thorough Mode" (45+ s: more alternatives and fuller explanations). **Quick Mode is faster, never less safe:** it never skips allergen, dietary-restriction, cart-coverage or arithmetic checks. Let users choose based on their priorities and available time. |

## Dimension 4: UX Friction & Human–AI Teaming

**Theme:** Long regeneration loops increase cognitive load. Users need targeted edits, not full-plan rewrites. Consequences of changes should be visible. Decision rights should be clear.

| Evidence | Observed Failure | Theoretical Interpretation | Design Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Interview 1 (Srinath, UIUC MSIM student):** "I'd want to see the whole plan and then say 'change Wednesday.' I wouldn't want to start over every time I don't like one meal." | User must regenerate entire weekly plan for a single meal swap. High cognitive load; user abandons. | **Attention and Interrogation Orchestration (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "When users are asked to interrogate AI outputs, they should do so at a level of detail and granularity that allows meaningful scrutiny." Asking a user to regenerate a 7-day plan to change 1 day overwhelms interrogation capacity. Users need surgical edits, not full rewrites. | **Implement targeted meal swaps:** User clicks "Replace Wednesday dinner." System generates 3 options for that day only, recalculates nutrition for that day, rebuilds the cart to reflect the change. Show: "New cart changes: [removed Y ingredient, added Z ingredient, net cost +$2]." Do not touch approved meals. |
| **Interview 7 (Sneha, working professional):** "I'd want to be able to lock certain preferences and then edit around them." | Users want hierarchical control: some things should stay fixed, others should flex. Current systems treat all constraints equally. | **Meta-Coordination (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** Role partitioning requires that humans decide what matters most. A user might say: "Lock my protein targets and Tuesday meals; optimize everything else around those." The AI should respect this priority hierarchy. | **Build a constraint-priority UI:** User ranks constraints in order of importance (Calories > Budget > Variety, or Budget > Variety > Calories). System optimizes to satisfy the top-ranked constraint first, then the next, etc. Show which constraints were satisfied and which were compromised. |
| **Copilot P2** ([Copilot_raw_outputs.md, line 367, 409–618](transcripts/raw-outputs/Copilot_raw_outputs.md)): Protein shakes were dropped from the cart "to control cost" without saying which meals lost them. | Cart edits don't cascade back to meals; the user must work out the consequences manually. | **Shared Mental Models (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** The AI and user must share understanding of cause-and-effect. When a user removes an ingredient from the cart, the system should show which meals lose that ingredient and suggest replacements. This builds a shared model of the plan's structure. | **On cart edit, flag meal impacts:** User removes Greek yogurt from cart. System shows: "Affects: Monday breakfast (loses 15g protein), Wednesday snack (loses 20g protein). Replace with: [options]." Require re-approval of the meal edit before finalizing. |
| **Copilot P1** ([Copilot_raw_outputs.md, line 280–328](transcripts/raw-outputs/Copilot_raw_outputs.md)): Returns only a cost estimate without a meal plan or cart. | User receives a summary with no supporting work to review or interrogate. | **Interrogation and Transparency (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Interfaces that expose uncertainty and rationales can strengthen shared mental models and improve calibration, communicating confidence and reasons for low confidence so that humans know when to scrutinize or defer." A bare cost estimate provides no scrutiny surface. Users cannot ask "Why?" or "Where?" | **Always provide itemized output:** meals, nutrition per day, ingredient list with quantities, cart rows with unit prices and subtotals. If the plan is incomplete or uncertain, say so explicitly: "Missing 3 ingredients (inventory check incomplete). Confidence: 60%. Approve partial plan?" |

## Dimension 5: Safety & Guardrails

**Theme:** AI asserts safety without evidence. Users expect escalation to themselves for high-stakes decisions. Allergens, dietary restrictions, and purchasing authority must remain under human control.

| Evidence | Observed Failure | Theoretical Interpretation | Design Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gemini P4** ([Gemini_raw_outputs.md, line 1743](transcripts/raw-outputs/Gemini_raw_outputs.md)): Asserts products are "completely free of peanuts and tree nuts" despite catalog lacking full ingredient and cross-contact information. Serves the plan as safe. | AI claims allergen safety without verified ingredient data or cross-contact information. | **Reasoning and Ethical Authority (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "AI systems cannot hold moral agency or be held responsible for harm. Humans must remain the locus of ethical authority and accountability in consequential decisions." Allergen safety is a consequence. The AI cannot claim safety it cannot verify; doing so usurps human authority. An allergen error can cause physical harm. | **Hard constraint:** Never claim "allergen-free" without a verified ingredient list + cross-contact info from the product manufacturer. If uncertain, escalate: "This product's allergen details are incomplete. We cannot verify tree-nut safety. Escalating to you for label review. Confirm before purchase?" Always defer to the human on allergen decisions. |
| **Gemini P5** ([Gemini_raw_outputs.md, line 1906](transcripts/raw-outputs/Gemini_raw_outputs.md)): Acknowledges curry kit is unlisted, labels its data "estimated," then serves it anyway in meals and cart. | AI acknowledges uncertainty but proceeds as if the problem is solved. | **Knowledge Infrastructure (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Hybrid knowledge systems in which AI retrieves, organizes, and cross-references information while humans validate, contextualize, and adjudicate are emerging as a robust model." Labeling invented data as "estimated" does not make it safe for use. Humans must adjudicate missing data. | **When a recipe ingredient is missing from the database, pause the workflow:** "Curry kit (C-unlisted) is not in our verified database. Options: (A) use [verified substitute, e.g., C19 Tikka Masala], (B) skip this meal, (C) you provide the product details (nutrition label photo, allergen info)." Never assume or estimate for food products. |
| **Interview 8 (Sneha, mid-career, family):** "I'd need the ingredient information to be accurate, especially for anything related to allergies or dietary restrictions. If it can't verify an ingredient, it should flag it. I don't want an AI making a guess where there could be a health consequence." | Users expect the AI to stop and ask, not guess, on safety-critical decisions. | **Accountability (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Humans are skilled at reconciling shifting stakeholder values but often struggle with value drift or inconsistent prioritization. AI systems can assist by tracking evolving constraints, focusing attention on high-impact decisions, and signaling inconsistencies." For families with allergies, ingredient safety is the highest-impact decision. The AI must escalate, not guess. | **Treat allergies and dietary restrictions as top-priority decision gates.** Every ingredient in every meal must have verified allergen info. If missing, flag the meal: "Day 3 lunch contains [ingredient with unknown allergen status]. Cannot verify safety. Approve skipping, or provide allergen info?" |
| **Interviews 3–6 (Chantrice, Kanishka) and the prompting protocol:** All four participants in the reflected notes wanted to review the cart before anything was ordered; no model was allowed to check out. | Users retain authority over purchase decisions; no auto-checkout under any circumstance. | **Meta-Coordination and Role Partitioning (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Humans design the overarching team architecture, including escalation paths, decision rights, and division of labor, while AI upholds procedural reliability through monitoring, workflow coordination, and auditability." Checkout is a high-stakes, consequential decision. It must remain in human hands. | **Hard gate:** Cart approval is mandatory and separate from plan approval. Show: "Review cart: [19 items, $196.81, 3 substitutions flagged]. (A) Approve and proceed to checkout, (B) make changes." No auto-checkout under any scenario, even if user is idle. Checkout requires explicit final action from the user on the order-confirmation page. |

## Dimension 6: Cost & Efficiency

**Theme:** Cost arithmetic errors undermine trust. Ingredient reuse and waste reduction matter more to users than rock-bottom prices. Pantry awareness is essential for efficiency.

| Evidence | Observed Failure | Theoretical Interpretation | Design Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Copilot P3, Copilot P5, Gemini P3:** Cart subtotals off by $0.98–$1.00; line items don't sum to reported total. | Arithmetic errors in cost calculations; even $1 error breaks trust in the entire cost estimate. | **Reasoning (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "AI systems excel at systematic checks and consistency monitoring but may fail silently with high confidence when encountering unfamiliar conditions." Arithmetic should be deterministic and verifiable. When totals don't match, users distrust all estimates. | **Move all cost arithmetic to deterministic code (Python/SQL), not LLM.** Generate line items with the LLM; calculate cart subtotals in software. Verify the subtotal twice independently. Report: "Subtotal: $196.81 (verified)" vs. "Estimated: $196.81 (±2%)." Show the calculation: "19 items × unit prices = $196.81 (breakdown available on hover)." |
| **Interviews 3 and 4 (Chantrice):** Both participants preferred fewer ingredients and repeated meals to lower cost; a 15-ingredient recipe did not feel "easy". | Users prioritize ingredient reuse and waste reduction over maximum variety. | **Attention (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "AI provides scanning, prioritization, misinformation filtering, and early-warning projections. Humans contribute broad contextual understanding, pattern novelty detection, and judgment about when and how to redirect collective attention." Users want the AI to focus on *their* priority (fewer ingredients, less waste), not on maximizing variety. The system should make this priority explicit. | **Add a cost-optimization mode:** "Prioritize: (A) Ingredient reuse (fewer unique items), (B) Variety (different meals), (C) Price (cheapest items)." If (A), enforce a max N unique ingredients per week and reuse aggressively. Show ingredient utilization: "Chicken used in 4 meals; 95% of purchased chicken will be consumed." |
| **Interview 1 (Srinath):** "If I already have rice and onions, I don't want the app telling me to buy more." | Not accounting for pantry inventory wastes money and breaks trust in the system's understanding of the user's situation. | **Memory and Transactive Memory (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** "Transactive memory systems enable teams to more quickly access diverse expertise and perform better. Well-developed transactive memory enables teams to more quickly access diverse expertise... but many human teams underutilize available knowledge, overlook teammates' expertise, or overrely on external searches." The AI's memory of what the user already has is critical. Buying duplicate items wastes money and signals the AI doesn't know the user's context. | **Add a pantry input step before cart generation.** User provides: "You have: rice, onions, olive oil, salt, eggs." System shows: "We'll use these in: [7 meals]. Not purchasing again." Remove these from cart. At checkout, re-confirm: "Your pantry inventory: [rice, onions]. Final cart excludes these. Confirm?" |
| **Copilot P2 and Gemini P5:** Missing key ingredients from cart make the plan unexecutable. User has to shop again. | Incomplete carts require a second trip; cost-inefficient plan. | **Attention and Reasoning (Gonzalez et al., 2026):** A cost-efficient plan that's missing ingredients is not cost-efficient—the user wastes time and money on a second trip. The system's reasoning must be end-to-end: meals → all required ingredients in cart → ready to execute. | **Enforce cart-to-meal coverage check:** Every ingredient in every meal must be in the cart in sufficient quantity. Run a verification script: **100% coverage is required for "cart ready"**; anything less is reported as a partial cart that needs a look (≥95% is only a research benchmark for model performance, never a ready state). If coverage < 100%, stop: "Missing ingredients for 2 meals: [Day 3 lunch needs spinach, Day 5 dinner needs lentils]. Add to cart? (A) Yes, add missing items, (B) remove these meals, (C) substitute." Do not present incomplete carts as ready. |

## Summary: Cognitive Pillars → Design Implications

| Cognitive Pillar | Key Gaps | Primary Design Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Memory** | Hallucinated products, prices, nutrition; no provenance; forgotten ingredients | Validate all data against trusted sources; attach provenance tags; flag estimates vs. confirmed; verify cart-to-meal coverage |
| **Reasoning** | Feasibility claims despite conflicts; unsupported safety assertions; constraint changes without approval | Explicit approval gates for conflicts; hard constraints (allergies, no-pantry, budget limits) are immutable; escalate unresolved trade-offs to user |
| **Attention** | Long regeneration loops; no targeted edits; latency variability; unclear priorities | Single-meal swaps; targeted edits; show downstream consequences; visible progress during generation; expose quality-vs.-speed trade-off |
| **Meta-Coordination** | Unclear decision rights; AI proceeds despite conflicts; locked preferences are changed; checkout authority ambiguous | Clear role boundaries (AI drafts, human approves); hard gates for allergies and purchasing; lock mechanisms for user preferences; mandatory cart approval before checkout |
